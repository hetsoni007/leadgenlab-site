A good choice depends on the jobs you actually need help with. Use this practical checklist to compare tools without getting distracted by feature lists. This guide is designed to give you the context first, then help you decide what questions to ask next.

Start with your real task

Write down two or three things you would use an assistant for: drafting, learning, summarizing, brainstorming, or organizing. Try the same representative task in each tool you are considering.

A tool that excels at your everyday work may be more useful than one with the longest feature list.

Check the quality of answers

Look for clear reasoning, useful structure, and a willingness to say when it is unsure. For research tasks, check whether it identifies sources you can inspect. Verify any important claim independently.

Understand privacy and access

Read what data the service stores and how it may be used. If you connect documents, email, or other accounts, check exactly what access is granted and how to revoke it.

Compare cost and fit

Consider the price after any trial, usage limits, supported devices, and integrations with tools you already use. Also check accessibility, language support, and whether you can export your work.

Make a small, reversible choice

Try one tool for a week with non-sensitive tasks. Keep a note of where it saved time and where you had to correct it. Then decide whether the benefit is worth the cost and data trade-off.

The short version: Stay curious, check the details that matter to you, and treat any tool recommendation as a starting point—not a substitute for your own judgment.
